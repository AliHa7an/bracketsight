Every other income-driven plan protects you from your own salary. The Repayment
Assistance Plan does not, and that single difference is what makes it the wrong
plan for a particular kind of borrower: high earner, moderate balance, no
forgiveness in sight.

<KeyFigure id="loans.rap.paymentCapped" variant="block" />

Income-Based Repayment computes a payment from your discretionary income and
then applies a ceiling: whatever the formula produces, you never pay more than
the 10-year Standard amount. RAP computes a payment from your **gross**
adjusted gross income — there is no poverty-line subtraction and no exempted
income at all — and applies no ceiling of any kind.

## How the RAP payment is built

RAP is a bracket table, not a percentage of discretionary income. Your AGI
lands in a <KeyFigure id="loans.rap.bracketStep" /> band, and the band names a
percentage of your whole AGI.

<FigureTable id="loans.rap.brackets" />

Two details that decide real cases. The regulation reads *"more than $X and
not more than $Y"*, so an AGI landing exactly on a boundary falls in the
**lower** band — $60,000 is 5%, not 6%. And the payment is reduced by
<KeyFigure id="loans.rap.dependentReduction" /> for each dependent you claim,
with a floor of <KeyFigure id="loans.rap.minimumMonthlyPayment" /> a month.

## Where it turns expensive

Follow a borrower earning $145,000 with a $42,000 balance at 6.53%.

RAP puts that AGI in the top band, <KeyFigure id="loans.rap.topRate" />, which
is about $1,208 a month. The 10-year Standard payment on the same balance is
roughly $477. Under IBR the ceiling would hold the payment at the Standard
amount; under RAP nothing holds it anywhere. The borrower pays two and a half
times as much per month, clears the balance years early, and reaches
<KeyFigure id="loans.rap.forgivenessPayments" /> having been forgiven nothing,
because there was nothing left to forgive.

That is not a defect in RAP. It is what an income-percentage plan does to
someone whose income is large relative to their debt, and it is precisely the
case the payment cap was invented for.

## Where RAP wins instead

Reverse the ratio and RAP is often the cheapest plan on the board, for two
reasons that have nothing to do with the payment formula.

The first is the interest waiver:
<KeyFigure id="loans.rap.interestWaiver" />. On an on-time payment, interest
that your payment did not cover is written off rather than added to the
balance. Under IBR, PAYE and ICR that interest accrues in a separate bucket and
follows you. Under RAP the balance cannot grow at all.

The second is the principal match. Every on-time payment reduces principal by
at least <KeyFigure id="loans.rap.principalMatch" /> — or by the payment
amount, when the payment is smaller than that. A borrower paying the
<KeyFigure id="loans.rap.minimumMonthlyPayment" /> minimum still moves the
balance down, which is a thing no other income-driven plan does.

So the shape of the answer is: **low income against a large balance, RAP is
usually the cheapest plan you can be on. High income against a moderate
balance, it can be the most expensive.** The crossover depends on your exact
numbers, which is what the simulation is for.

## The trap on the way in

Switching to RAP forfeits every qualifying payment you have already made under
IBR, PAYE or ICR. Those payments do not transfer. A borrower eleven years into
old IBR — <KeyFigure id="loans.ibrOld.forgivenessPayments" /> to forgiveness —
who moves to RAP restarts at zero against
<KeyFigure id="loans.rap.forgivenessPayments" />. That is a one-way door, and
it is the single most expensive mistake available in the whole system.

There is a related trap on the way through. Paying **extra** on RAP does not
simply pay the loan down faster: amounts above the required payment go to
interest first and can cancel both the waiver and the principal match for that
month. The plan rewards paying exactly what is asked.

## What happens at the end

Forgiveness under RAP arrives at <KeyFigure id="loans.rap.forgivenessPayments" />.
Under Public Service Loan Forgiveness it arrives at
<KeyFigure id="loans.pslf.payments" /> instead, and the difference between
those two endpoints is a tax bill:
<KeyFigure id="loans.tax.nonPslfForgivenessTaxable" variant="block" />

PSLF discharge is <KeyFigure id="loans.tax.pslfForgivenessTaxable" />. Any
ranking that ignores the tax on a non-PSLF forgiven balance is ranking the
wrong number, which is why the simulation carries it.
