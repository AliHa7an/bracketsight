Most tax thresholds taper. You cross one, and a benefit shrinks by a few cents
on the dollar. The premium tax credit ceiling is not one of those. Cross it by
a single dollar and the entire credit is gone — not reduced, gone — and if you
took it in advance during the year, you pay all of it back.

<KeyFigure id="aca.cliff.ceilingMultiple" variant="block" />

## Why there is no grace band

The natural assumption is that a rule expressed in whole percentages must have
a little slack in it: if the limit is 400%, surely 400.4% rounds down. It does
not, and the reason is the order of operations on Form 8962.

Worksheet 2 asks first whether household income is **more than** the poverty
line multiplied by 4.0. If the answer is yes, you enter
<KeyFigure id="aca.cliff.ineligibleSentinel" /> on line 5 and stop. The
whole-percent truncation that governs every interior boundary — 133%, 150%,
200%, 250%, 300% — is only reached in the "no" branch. So the last eligible
household income is exactly four times the poverty line, to the dollar.

For a single-person household in the 48 contiguous states, the poverty figure
the 2026 coverage year uses is <KeyFigure id="aca.fpl.firstPerson" />, plus
<KeyFigure id="aca.fpl.additionalPerson" /> for each additional person. Note
which year that is: marketplace eligibility for a coverage year uses the
guidelines in effect when open enrolment began, which is the **previous**
calendar year's table. Using the current year's figures moves the cliff by
several hundred dollars and puts households on the wrong side of it.

## What the credit is worth right up to the edge

Below the ceiling, the credit is the gap between the benchmark plan's premium
and what the law says your household should be able to afford. That expected
contribution rises with income across six bands.

<FigureTable id="aca.applicablePercentage.bands" />

At the top of the range a household is expected to pay
<KeyFigure id="aca.applicablePercentage.top" /> of its income toward the
benchmark plan, and the credit covers everything above that. One dollar later
the expected contribution is 100% of the premium. Nothing in the table
describes that step, because the step is not in the table — it is the table
ending.

## The part that catches people after the fact

The credit is usually taken in advance, paid straight to the insurer month by
month, based on the income you estimated at enrolment. Reconciliation happens
on the following year's return. Estimate low and you owe the difference.

<KeyFigure id="aca.clawback.capped" variant="block" />

Until tax year 2025 there was a repayment limitation: a household under 400%
of the poverty line that had underestimated its income repaid at most a capped
amount. P.L. 119-21 § 71305 struck that provision for tax years beginning after
31 December 2025. There is now no cap at any income. A household that took
$9,000 of advance credit and finished the year one dollar over the ceiling
repays the whole $9,000.

That change is why the cliff is worth more attention than it used to be. The
edge was always there; the airbag underneath it was removed.

## What can still be done about it

The credit is computed on modified adjusted gross income, and MAGI is not a
number handed to you — it is the result of decisions, several of which are
still open after the year has ended. A deductible traditional IRA contribution,
an HSA contribution, a self-employed retirement plan contribution: each reduces
MAGI directly, and near the ceiling each dollar of reduction is worth far more
than its marginal tax value, because it is buying back a credit that is
all-or-nothing.

The arithmetic that matters is not "how much tax does this save" but "does this
land the household under four times its poverty figure". Those are different
questions with different answers.

## Cost-sharing reductions fall off a different ledge

The premium tax credit is not the only thing income decides. Silver-plan
cost-sharing reductions — lower deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums, not
lower premiums — end at 250% of the poverty line, and they are worth a great
deal at the bottom of the range.

<FigureTable id="aca.csr.bands" />

A household between 250% and 400% has premium help and no cost-sharing help.
One between 150% and 200% has both, and its silver plan behaves approximately
like a platinum one. Two ledges, at different heights, and the second is the
one people are usually unaware of.
